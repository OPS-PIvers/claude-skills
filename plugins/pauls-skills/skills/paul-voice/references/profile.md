# Paul's voice profile

Everything here comes from text Paul wrote or said himself. Assistant turns, quoted or forwarded text, and drafts other people wrote were left out. Quotes are short and lightly trimmed, and nothing here names a student, a colleague or anyone in his family.

Tags show how sure each line is. **Seen** means many samples across sources. **Few** means two or three. **Guess** means inferred, so check it against the next real sample.

## 1. Dimensions

- **Formality.** Plain and friendly, a teacher talking to colleagues. Formal pieces like an application essay get longer sentences and more care, but the vocabulary stays ordinary. Seen.
- **Sentence length.** Typed prose runs medium to long, with compound sentences chained by "and" and "so". Email replies are short, often one to three sentences. Speech averages about 15 words. Chat is often one short line. Seen.
- **Contractions.** Yes, everywhere except the most formal writing. Seen.
- **Vocabulary reach.** Ordinary words used precisely. Real product names are fine (Schoology, Synergy, ClassLink, Gemini, SpartBoard, Workspace Studio). Education buzzwords are not. Seen.
- **Addresses the reader.** Yes, second person, constantly. "You can...", "If you haven't...", "Come and join us". Seen.
- **Hedging.** Soft and honest rather than salesy. "Hopefully", "my hope is", "should be" for a result he expects but hasn't watched happen ("should be good to go", "should be snappier"), "my guess is", "I *think*" with the word in italics, "I'm not 100% sure", "kind of", "a little bit", "pretty". Seen.
- **Confidence.** When he is sure, he says "certainly" ("it's certainly all possible", "certainly worth double checking"). It's his counterweight to the hedges. Few.
- **Exclamation points.** In email, one on the greeting ("Good morning!") and one on the closing line, plus now and then one short upbeat line in the body ("They are also learning a new system!"). Never two in a row, never "!!". Subject lines sometimes end in one. Almost none in long-form docs, and none across a 15k-word spoken PD session. Seen.
- **Emoji.** Rare. An occasional grin after a joke in a quick one-to-one reply to a colleague he knows, and Gmail reactions. Never in staff-wide, admin or outside mail. Copy written for him leaves emoji out unless he asks, and deslop's emoji rule still applies. Seen.

## 2. Characteristic moves

- **Gets to the ask fast.** A request is often the first sentence ("Could you give me the details about...", "Whenever you have a moment, could you look into..."). The cohort email thanks people in one sentence and goes straight to what's due. Seen.
- **Leads a reply with the result.** "The bug is fixed now." "I set up the class." "Should be good to go moving forward." Then what was going on, then the one thing the reader has to do. Seen.
- **Does it for them, then says what he did.** Support replies list what he found and what he already changed on their account, numbered, with the fix after each item. Seen.
- **Puts the time cost up front.** "Please take 30 seconds to fill out...", "it should take ~5 minutes or so". Few.
- **Lays out the real case before asking a leader to decide.** A short concrete scenario (who did what, today's numbers), his honest read ("my gut reaction is...") with the benefit of the doubt for the people involved, then one direct question set in bold, the tradeoff, and an offer to talk in person. Seen.
- **Organizes action by urgency.** "Do now", "Do next", "On the radar", with the due date in bold. "What this means for you:" as a bold lead-in before the steps. Few.
- **Frames outcomes as hopes, not promises.** "...so hopefully they are able to use those when they are on their own." "In the hopes that they will also be learning strategies from my approach." "My hope is that you are able to just copy and paste..." Seen.
- **Owns a slip right away.** One short line, then the fix. "Sorry about that!", "Sorry, I wish I knew more definitively!", or an apology for a runaround he didn't cause. Seen.
- **Proves a point with a real classroom moment.** His application essay argues for AI ethics teaching with one specific story about students noticing gender bias in an image generator. In PD he says what he did as a teacher ("I can speak to the high school as I'm still teaching a high school course"). Seen.
- **Dry, self-deprecating humor with a flat payoff.** A straight setup, then a short undercut aimed at himself. "And you don't have to speak as long as me because I talk too much." "...pretty goofy, but we'll give her a go." From a speech, roughly, "one of the two drummers in that photo became a professional orchestra musician. The other one is me." Seen. Only use it when Paul gave you the material.
- **Wry asides in email.** A half-line of shared frustration or a tease, then back to business. "Fun answer, right?" after an unsatisfying answer, a mock-serious question to a language teacher about their spelling, a long software gripe that ends on the absurd order classes come out in. Seen. Same rule, his material only.
- **Opens a newsletter tip with the reader's frustration.** A rhetorical question in the reader's voice ("What do I even do with this?"), a too-real detail, then the fix. Few.
- **Leads people somewhere with "So".** In speech about a quarter of sentences start with "So". In writing it shows up as "So, to welcome you back..." or "So, I was on Spotify the other day..." Seen in speech, few in writing.
- **Signposts in plain words.** "That said,", "Additionally,", "Essentially,", "To fix this,", "Going forward". Seen in email.
- **Reframes instead of contradicting.** "I don't think of that as a negative." Few.
- **Pairs a contrast to make a point stick.** "Proactive instead of reactive... a forethought instead of an afterthought." Few. Careful here, since deslop bans the "not X, but Y" antithesis, so keep his pairs literal and don't manufacture new ones.
- **Short, flat praise.** "I love it. It's great." "That's great! Way to troubleshoot." Seen.
- **Talks about the future self.** Benefits framed as "looking out for your future self" or a "better teacher experience as well". Few.
- **Names the person to contact and offers help.** Closes with an open door ("Let me know if you have any questions.", "Let me know if that works or what needs to be tweaked"), an offer to meet in person, or a session with the time and place spelled out. Seen.

## 3. Registers

### Staff-wide email (broadcasts to a building or a group)

- Greeting to the group with a comma or one exclamation point. "Good morning all,", "Hi OMS,", "Hi ILT,". Seen.
- First sentence says what this is and what it costs them, often with the time. Then the steps, numbered, with on-screen labels in bold. Seen.
- Uses "we" for the team doing the fixing and treats problems as normal shakedown ("ironing out some wrinkles", "slowly getting the wrinkles out of the process"). Seen.
- One practical extra at the end is common, like a suggestion for what to remind students. Few.
- Closes with "Let me know if you have any questions." and his name. Seen.

### Tech Tips newsletter

- Opens with a short "we're back" line in "we", the goal of the tips ("as quick (and painless) as possible to jump in and try something new"), and what this issue covers. Seen.
- Tip blurbs are two or three short plain sentences each, the steps bulleted with the button names in bold, then a link to a guide or live tour. Seen.
- No sign-off. The footer carries the team name. Seen.
- The product blurbs in recent issues read team-written and more neutral than his intros, so take his voice from the intros and the problem-first hooks, not from feature descriptions. Few.

### One-to-one staff email and quick replies

- Greeting is "Good morning!" most often, "Hey [first name]," for people he works with closely, "Hi [first name]," otherwise. A comma after a name, the exclamation goes on "Good morning". Seen.
- A reply in an ongoing thread often skips the greeting and the name and starts with the answer. Seen.
- One to three sentences when that's enough. Result first, then the cause in plain words, then what they do next. Seen.
- Lists their options as "A) ... or B)" inside the sentence, and numbers longer fixes "1)", "2)". Seen.
- Closes with "Hope this helps", "Let me know!", or an offer to connect in person. Note that deslop flags "I hope this helps!" as an AI tell, so in copy written for him prefer the open door. Seen.
- Sign-off is "Paul" alone on its own line, with "Thanks!" or "Thank you!" on the line above when he's asking for something. No "Best," and no title block. Seen.
- Introductions and hand-offs are one or two sentences. Who will help, that he cc'd them, and an offer to back them up. Seen.

### Email to building and district leaders

- Same plain voice as staff email, with more candor and a direct question. Seen.
- Lays out the situation as a concrete case before asking, says what he tried and why it backfired, and names the tradeoff he sees. Seen.
- One question in bold, then "Happy to connect in person today if that's easier!" or similar. Seen.
- When he's offering several technical options, he nests bullets with the condition first ("If it's not time-sensitive..."), and states the cost or the constraint on each one. Few.

### Email to vendors and outside partners

- "Hi [first name]," and a line of appreciation that sounds like a person ("I appreciate you reaching out proactively", "Really appreciate the follow up"). Seen.
- Then the facts with numbers and dates, and direct questions. "Can we circle back then?", "Are you able to help with this or direct me to someone who can?" Seen.
- Corrections are one line and cheerful ("Update... apparently the number is now 35. Sorry about that!"). Few.
- Thank-yous to outside partners are one or two warm sentences and a personal send-off. Few.
- Never "I hope this email finds you well" or "at your earliest convenience" (he writes "whenever you have a moment"). Seen, by their absence across all of his mail while the vendors use them.

### Formal email (sensitive follow-ups, decisions)

- "Good morning," with a comma and no exclamation points anywhere. Few.
- Says why he's writing in the first line ("I wanted to follow up to close the loop about..."), states the outcome plainly, and thanks the reader twice, once for the conversation and once for their support. Few.
- Short. No backstory the reader already has. Few.

### Slide copy and speaker notes

- Slides carry a title and a few words. The sentences go in the notes or callouts.
- Speaker notes sound like him talking. Short setups, a pause, then the line. Ellipses mark the pause in his own notes, and he writes `[CLICK]` cues.
- Personal stories are welcome here and are his strongest material, but they come from him.

### PD guides, onboarding copy, how-tos

- Second person, present tense, one step per sentence. "Click **Customize** in Claude's sidebar."
- Bold only the thing on screen the reader has to find.
- Plain reasons attached to rules. "Student work counts even with the name taken off, because the details in it can still point to a student."
- Honest about what's unknown. "After that, we do not know the cost, nor whether Claude adds more than what we already have."

### Formal writing (applications, policy, board-facing)

- Longer, more careful sentences, with qualifiers like "of course", "possibly equally if not more importantly", "Lastly". Seen in one long sample.
- Still argues from one concrete example rather than a list of credentials. He struck out a whole AI-drafted section of bullet-point qualifications in his own application.
- Values words he actually uses here are relationships, collaboration, listening, lifelong learner, humility, "our best work is done together".

### Quick chat messages

- Short, often one line, sometimes lowercase. "Ha yeah", "Oh definitely", "def", "legit", "I mean", "honestly", "ya know?" as a closer.
- Asks for your opinion directly. "What do you think is best?" "Do you agree? If not, what is your perspective?"
- When drafting a chat or text he'll send, keep the casual words but fix the typos. He leaves transposed letters in his own typing, and copy written for him shouldn't copy that.

### Dev and code chat, PR text he signs

- Pastes the error, then asks a plain question about it.
- Talks about goals as a dream ("in my dream world...") and is open that he builds with AI rather than writing code by hand.
- Approvals are terse ("yes build it all"), and so is admitting a miss ("Alright, I was wrong.").
- PR descriptions follow the repo's own template. The voice applies to the prose, not the structure.

## 4. What he cuts from AI drafts

From his own corrections and from the AI-drafted section he struck out of his application.

- Marketing verbs and buzzwords. "Empower", "leverage" (in writing), "actionable", "unlock", "harness", "revolutionize", "game changer", "cutting-edge", "robust", "pivotal", "underscores", "unique blend", "journey". Seen.
- Promises of saved time or transformation. He corrected a draft that called core tools "time-savers", because the real point was getting more proficient. Few.
- Clever or abstract titles. "I really don't like those titles." Few.
- Bubbly tone and exclamation runs. "That's a bit too bubbly." Few.
- Polite outs like "totally fine if it's not your thing". Few.
- Sterile, generic prose that could have come from any district. "It just feels like AI wrote it and doesn't sound like me, so it's pretty sterile." Seen.
- Vague claims with no detail behind them. "There's like very little detail about what the activities even were." Few.
- Stock email furniture he never writes himself. "I hope this email finds you well", "at your earliest convenience", "Best," or "Sincerely," before his name, a signature block. Seen in his mail by absence.

## 6. Typing habits (observe, don't copy)

These show up in what he types but don't belong in copy written for him. They're here so nobody mistakes them for style.

- Two spaces after a period.
- `--` or ` - ` where a dash would go, `--or--` between two options, and `-->` to point at the fix or the result of a step. Deslop bans dashes in prose, and that ban wins. Write the pointer as a numbered step or "then" instead.
- Ellipses typed as four or five unspaced dots ("....") for a beat of doubt or exasperation. In casual copy written for him, use three.
- Transposed-letter typos, dropped letters, and the occasional lowercase "i".
- CAPS for emphasis in frustrated chat ("it is STILL saying...") and once or twice in email to stress one word ("AND", "DO"). In copy written for him, use bold or italics on the one word instead.
- A trailing `--` under his name from Gmail's signature delimiter. Not part of the sign-off.
